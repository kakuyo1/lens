Attention and the Reading Mind

Mara Ellison, Jonas Reiter
Institute of Reading Studies · Working paper 14 · October 2026

Abstract. Reading is not one act but a queue of small decisions: where to look, which word to trust, when to stop. This paper follows those decisions through a year of screen reading, and asks what a reader does with a word that is still out of reach.

1 · Introduction

Reading on a screen has become ubiquitous, and the eye moves over text that is often read once and never returned to. A page that would once have been marked in pencil now passes by untouched, so the words a reader does not yet own leave no trace at all.

Not every word in a paragraph is equally salient. A rare word draws the eye while the common ones pass unnoticed, and the difference is measurable in the first two hundred milliseconds of a fixation. Attention, in turn, is attenuated by fatigue, and the effect is strongest late in a session, when a reader is least able to notice that a word was missed.

2 · What readers do instead

Readers fall back on a heuristic: skip the unknown word and let the sentence carry the meaning. It works often enough to be worth keeping, and it fails quietly — a paragraph is understood, a term is not, and nothing in the reading experience records the difference.

The problem is pervasive in academic reading, where one unfamiliar term can hold a whole paragraph in place. Marking such words as they appear, rather than after the fact, is the intervention this paper tests.
